Swarm intelligence: from ants to LLMs

The word “swarm” in your project's name comes from biology, where it means something precise: many simple individuals, no leader, local rules, and a useful global pattern that nobody planned. This chapter teaches the real mechanisms (flocking, pheromone trails, particle swarms), then checks honestly which of them carry over to a handful of expensive LLM agents.

What “swarm intelligence” actually means

The term is usually credited to Gerardo Beni and Jing Wang (1989), who used it for groups of simple robots. Bonabeau, Dorigo and Theraulaz's book Swarm Intelligence (1999) made it a field. The definition has three parts, and all three matter:

  • Many simple individuals. Each follows a few rules and has no model of the whole system.
  • Only local information. Each senses its neighbors or its immediate surroundings, not the global state.
  • No central control. Nobody assigns tasks or holds the plan. The useful behavior emerges from interactions.

Emergence means a property of the group that no individual has and no rule mentions. No ant's rule says “find the shortest path”. No bird's rule says “form a V”. The pattern exists only at the level of the group.

Bonabeau and colleagues named four ingredients of this kind of self-organization. Use them as a checklist whenever someone calls a system a swarm:

IngredientWhat it doesIn an ant colony
Positive feedbackAmplifies good optionsMore ants on a trail → more pheromone → more ants
Negative feedbackStops runaway amplificationPheromone evaporates; a food source runs out; trails get crowded
RandomnessKeeps exploring, and can find new optionsSome ants wander off-trail and occasionally find a new source
Multiple interactionsSpreads information through the groupEvery ant both reads and writes the trail network

Boids: three rules make a flock

In 1987 Craig Reynolds, a computer graphics researcher, wanted realistic flocks for animation without scripting every bird. His “boids” (bird-oids) each follow three steering rules, computed only from neighbors within a small radius:

One boid, one time step (pseudocode)

def step(boid, flock, r=50, w_sep=1.5, w_ali=1.0, w_coh=1.0):
    near = [b for b in flock if b is not boid and dist(b, boid) < r]   # local only
    if not near:
        return boid.pos + boid.vel
    sep = sum(boid.pos - b.pos for b in near if dist(b, boid) < r / 3)  # push apart
    ali = mean(b.vel for b in near) - boid.vel                          # match heading
    coh = mean(b.pos for b in near) - boid.pos                          # pull together
    boid.vel = limit(boid.vel + w_sep*sep + w_ali*ali + w_coh*coh, max_speed)
    return boid.pos + boid.vel

Three things to notice, because they recur in every swarm algorithm:

  • Each rule alone is useless. Cohesion alone collapses into a clump. Separation alone scatters. Alignment alone gives parallel lines that drift apart. The flock exists only in the balance, which lives in three weights.
  • The weights are the design. Change w_sep from 1.5 to 0.2 and you get a different, often broken, behavior. Emergent systems are tuned, not specified, and the tuning is found by experiment.
  • Locality is a feature. The radius r keeps each boid's input small and keeps the group robust: lose any one bird and the flock doesn't notice.

Ant colonies, stigmergy and ant colony optimization

Ants coordinate without talking to each other directly. They change the environment, and the changed environment tells other ants what to do. Pierre-Paul Grassé named this stigmergy in 1959, while studying how termites build nests. Foraging ants lay a chemical trail, pheromone, as they walk. Other ants tend to follow stronger trails. The trail is a message left in a place, not sent to anyone.

Experiments by Deneubourg, Goss and colleagues around 1989–1990 connected a nest to food by two bridges of different lengths. At first ants split between the bridges at random. Ants on the short bridge got back sooner, so pheromone built up faster there, so more ants chose it. Within minutes most traffic used the short bridge. No ant compared the two lengths.

From ants to an algorithm

Marco Dorigo turned this into ant colony optimization (ACO) in his 1992 PhD thesis. The first version, Ant System, was published by Dorigo, Maniezzo and Colorni in 1996 and applied to the travelling salesman problem. Artificial ants build solutions step by step on a graph. Two rules drive everything.

Rule 1, choosing the next edge. An ant at node i picks an edge to node j with a probability that combines the edge's pheromone τ (what the colony has learned) with a heuristic η (a local hint, such as 1/distance):

p(i→j) = τijα · ηijβ  /  Σallowed k τikα · ηikβ
α = how much to trust the colony's trail · β = how much to trust the local hint

Rule 2, updating the trails. After every ant has built a solution, all trails evaporate by a fraction ρ, and each ant adds pheromone to the edges it used, more for shorter tours:

τij ← (1 − ρ) · τij + Σants k that used ij Q / Lk
ρ = evaporation rate (negative feedback) · Q / Lk = deposit, larger for a shorter tour (positive feedback)

Worked example: watch the colony decide

Two paths from nest to food: short (L = 1) and long (L = 2). Ten ants per round. α = 1, and we ignore the heuristic (β = 0) so only pheromone matters. ρ = 0.5, Q = 1. Both trails start at τ = 1. To keep the arithmetic clean, use the expected number of ants on each path.

  • ρ = 0 (no evaporation): every early deposit stays forever. If the first rounds happen to favor a bad path by chance, the colony can lock onto it. This is premature convergence, the algorithm's version of groupthink.
  • ρ = 1 (total evaporation): the colony forgets everything after each round. There is no learning, and the ants just repeat the round-1 split.
Key idea

Stigmergy is coordination through a shared, decaying medium. The decay matters as much as the writing. A shared store that only ever accumulates (old claims, old findings, old votes) behaves like an ant colony with ρ = 0: it reinforces whatever got there first.

Particle swarm optimization

James Kennedy and Russell Eberhart introduced particle swarm optimization (PSO) in 1995. It started as a simulation of social behavior and turned out to be a good optimizer for continuous problems. Each particle is a candidate solution: a point x in the search space with a velocity v. Each particle remembers the best point it has found itself (pbest). The swarm shares the best point anyone has found (gbest). On every step, each particle updates its velocity from three pulls:

Read the equation term by term. Shi and Eberhart (1998) added the inertia weight w, and typical settings put w around 0.7 and c1, c2 around 1.5. Exact values vary by problem.

w·v (inertia)
Momentum. Large w means wide exploration. If w is above 1, velocities can grow without limit and the swarm flies apart.
c1·r1·(pbest − x) (memory)
The particle's own experience. With only this term, particles never share anything: you get many independent searches.
c2·r2·(gbest − x) (social)
The swarm's shared knowledge. With only this term, every particle rushes to the current best point. That converges fast, often to a local optimum, because nobody keeps looking elsewhere.

The trade-off between the last two terms is the trade-off of every collective search, including groups of people and groups of LLM agents. Too little sharing wastes effort on duplicate dead ends. Too much sharing gives premature convergence: everyone copies the early leader, and the group stops exploring. A widely used variant limits sharing on purpose. Each particle sees only the best point among a few neighbors (a local best) instead of the global best, which slows the spread of information and keeps diversity longer.

What decentralization buys, and what it costs

You get……and you pay with
Robustness. No single point of failure. Losing individuals barely matters.Predictability. You can't easily say in advance what the group will do, or prove that it will finish.
Scalability. Each individual's work depends on its neighbors, not on the group size.Speed to a decision. Information spreads hop by hop. Convergence takes many rounds.
Adaptivity. When the environment changes, trails re-form without anyone re-planning.Controllability. To change the outcome you tune weights and rates, not instructions. Tuning is found by experiment.
Simple parts. Each individual is easy to build and test.Debuggability. No single trace explains the result. The cause is spread over thousands of interactions.

Notice where the benefits come from: very many individuals, very cheap steps, and many rounds. Hold on to that sentence. It decides almost everything in the rest of this chapter.

What “swarm” means in LLM systems

In LLM work, “swarm” is used loosely. Three quite different things carry the name, and it helps to keep them apart.

1 · Handoff networks: OpenAI's Swarm

In October 2024 OpenAI released Swarm, an experimental, educational framework built around two ideas it called routines and handoffs. A routine is an agent: instructions plus the functions it may call. A handoff is a function that returns another agent. When the model calls it, the loop swaps the active agent and keeps the conversation history. In March 2025 OpenAI replaced Swarm with the production-oriented OpenAI Agents SDK, which keeps handoffs as a core concept.

Look at what this is: a state machine whose states are agents and whose transitions are chosen by the model. It's useful, and it's the handoff pattern from Chapter 17. It has none of the four swarm ingredients. There is no feedback loop, no randomness used for exploration, and no many-to-many interaction. Only one agent is active at a time.

2 · Agent societies: simulations

Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) put 25 LLM-driven characters in a small simulated town called Smallville. Each one has a memory stream, periodically reflects on its memories, and makes plans. The famous result: the researchers gave one character the intention to host a Valentine's Day party. Over the following simulated days, invitations spread from character to character, others asked each other to the party, and several showed up at the right time and place. Nobody scripted the spread. That is genuine emergence, through many local conversations, much like Figure 18.1.

CAMEL (Li et al., 2023) paired two agents in a role-play: an “AI user” that gives instructions and an “AI assistant” that carries them out, with carefully designed starting prompts (“inception prompting”) to keep them on task. The paper is honest about what goes wrong when two LLMs talk without supervision: the agents swap roles, repeat instructions back to each other, give vague replies that do nothing, or loop on the same exchange. You'll meet these again as failure modes in Chapter 19.

Both are mainly research instruments, for studying social behavior or generating training conversations. Neither is a design for getting a task done cheaply and reliably.

3 · Debate and collective answers

In multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2023), several model instances answer a question, read each other's answers, and revise over a few rounds. The paper reported gains on arithmetic, reasoning and factuality tasks.

Here debate worked, because the majority was right and the error was checkable: agent-2 could see the missing division. Change those two conditions and it can fail. If two of three agents share the same wrong belief, the correct minority may give in, and the debate converges confidently on the wrong answer. Later evaluations, such as Smit et al. (2024), found that debate doesn't reliably beat simpler methods like self-consistency voting at a similar cost, and that results are sensitive to settings such as the number of agents and rounds. Treat debate as one more aggregator to measure, not as a guaranteed improvement.

What transfers to LLM agents, and what doesn't

Go back to the sentence from the strengths-and-costs section: swarm benefits come from very many individuals, very cheap steps, and many rounds. LLM agents fail all three. Row by row:

PropertyAnts, boids, particlesLLM agentsSo…
ScaleHundreds to millionsUsually 3 to 50No averaging over huge numbers. Each agent's mistake matters.
Cost per stepNearly freeA model call: seconds and real moneyYou can't afford wasteful random exploration.
RoundsThousandsA handfulNo time to converge by slow reinforcement. Aggregate explicitly.
Individual2–3 fixed rulesA general reasoner with long instructionsBehavior can't be predicted from rules, and is less stable.
IndependenceRandom individual variationCopies of one model share biasesErrors are correlated (Chapter 17). Diversity must be designed in.
LocalitySense only neighborsOften read a large shared contextEveryone sees the same thing early, so herding comes easily.
StigmergyPheromone that evaporatesA blackboard or shared storeTransfers well, if entries expire and carry evidence, not just popularity.
RobustnessAny individual is replaceableAn agent may hold a unique subtaskRedundancy costs tokens. Retry or reassign instead.
Feedback signalObjective: path length, fitnessOften another model's judgmentThe signal you reinforce is itself noisy (Chapter 13).

The ideas that survive the trip are the mechanisms, not the scale: a shared medium with decay (stigmergy as a blackboard with expiry), explicit balance between exploiting the best answer and exploring alternatives (PSO's memory vs social terms), and negative feedback that stops runaway amplification (evaporation, caps, budgets). What doesn't survive is the hope that useful behavior will emerge by itself from many cheap trials.

A spectrum, not a binary

Real systems sit somewhere between a single planner and a leaderless swarm. Where you sit is a choice about what you're willing to give up.

An honest note on the word

Many products and repositories called “agent swarms” are orchestrator-worker systems or handoff networks with a biological name. That isn't wrong. It just isn't swarm intelligence in Bonabeau's sense. When you read about one, run the four-ingredient checklist: where is the positive feedback, the negative feedback, the randomness, the many-to-many interaction? If you can't find them, evaluate it as an orchestrator, because that's what it is. The same applies to your own project, and that's fine: a well-bounded tree is a perfectly good thing to build.

Misconceptions to drop
  • “More agents means emergent intelligence.” Emergence needs many cheap interactions and feedback loops. Five expensive agents talking a few times won't produce it.
  • “Swarms are robust, so an LLM swarm is robust.” Robustness comes from interchangeable individuals. LLM agents usually aren't interchangeable.
  • “Agreement among agents is a strong signal.” In ACO without evaporation, and in debate among correlated models, agreement can just be early luck reinforced.
  • “OpenAI's Swarm is a swarm.” It's a handoff state machine. Useful, but a different thing.
In your swarm harness
  • Naming. Run the four-ingredient checklist on your own design as it stands after Stage 6. Which ingredients does it have? If the answer is “none”, is that a problem for your goals, or only for the name?
  • Stage 7–8, a blackboard. If you add a shared findings or claims store, what plays the role of evaporation? Who removes stale entries, and when? What happens to a claim whose owner crashed?
  • Evidence vs popularity. If an entry's “strength” goes up each time another agent confirms it, are you measuring evidence or herding? What would an agent have to attach to count as an independent confirmation?
  • Exploration vs exploitation. Which part of your tree plays PSO's social term, pulling everyone toward the current best plan? Which part, if any, keeps exploring alternatives?
  • A research angle. Your harness can run controlled experiments that the papers in this chapter couldn't afford at scale. Write down one hypothesis, for example about how diverse sub-task decompositions affect correlated errors. What would you measure, and against which baseline?

Check yourself

Answer each one out loud or on paper before you open it.

Recall Name the four ingredients of self-organization, with an ant-colony example of each.

Positive feedback: more ants on a trail leave more pheromone, which attracts more ants. Negative feedback: pheromone evaporates, and food sources run out. Randomness: some ants wander and discover new sources. Multiple interactions: every ant reads and writes the shared trail network, so information spreads through the colony.

Recall State the three boids rules. What does each boid need to sense to apply them?

Separation: steer away from neighbors that are too close. Alignment: steer toward the neighbors' average heading. Cohesion: steer toward the neighbors' average position. Each boid needs only the positions and velocities of neighbors within a small radius. It never sees the whole flock.

Apply Continue the worked example for one more round, from τshort ≈ 12.0 and τlong ≈ 2.94. Then say what happens with ρ = 0 and with ρ = 1.

p(short) = 12.0 / 14.94 ≈ 0.80, so about 8.0 ants go short and 2.0 go long. τshort = 0.5·12.0 + 8.0 ≈ 14.0. τlong = 0.5·2.94 + 2.0/2 ≈ 2.46. p(short) for the next round ≈ 14.0 / 16.5 ≈ 0.85. With ρ = 0 nothing evaporates, so early random imbalances persist and the colony can lock onto a bad path. With ρ = 1 all memory is erased each round, so the split stays at 0.50 forever and nothing is learned.

Apply In PSO, what happens if you set c1 = 0 and make c2 large? What if w is above 1?

With c1 = 0 and a large c2, particles ignore their own memory and rush toward the current global best. The swarm converges fast, often on a local optimum, because nobody keeps exploring: premature convergence. With w above 1, velocity grows each step unless it's clamped, so particles overshoot more and more and the swarm diverges.

Design Map stigmergy onto a blackboard for LLM research agents. What are the deposit, the evaporation and the trail strength? Name one risk.

Deposit: an agent writes a finding or a claim, with its source and the agent's id. Evaporation: entries expire after a time to live unless renewed, and claims lapse if the owner stops heartbeating. Strength: the number of independent confirmations, each with its own source. Risks: herding, where agents confirm whatever is already on the board because they all read it first; injected text spreading to every reader; and an unbounded board flooding every agent's context.

Interview “Is OpenAI's Swarm framework an example of swarm intelligence?”

Not in the technical sense. Swarm is a handoff network: agents are instructions plus functions, and a function can return another agent, which becomes the active one with the history kept. It's a state machine whose transitions the model chooses. Only one agent is active at a time, and there's no positive or negative feedback loop, no randomness used for exploration, and no many-to-many interaction. The name is a metaphor. Its replacement, the Agents SDK, keeps the handoff idea.

Interview “Would you use swarm intelligence ideas in an LLM-based research system?”

Selectively. The scale and cost assumptions don't transfer: LLM agents are few, expensive and correlated, so you can't rely on emergence from many cheap trials. Three mechanisms do transfer. A shared store with decay (stigmergy) helps avoid duplicate work and stale claims. Deliberately balancing shared knowledge against independent exploration reduces groupthink. Negative feedback, such as caps, budgets and expiring entries, stops runaway amplification. I'd keep central control over spawning and budgets, and measure any decentralized piece against a single-agent baseline.

Recall card

Five lines to rehearse. If you can expand each into a minute of explanation, you know this chapter.

  1. Swarm intelligence: many simple individuals, local information, no central control, and an emergent global pattern.
  2. Self-organization needs positive feedback, negative feedback, randomness and multiple interactions.
  3. Boids: separation, alignment, cohesion. ACO: probabilistic choice by pheromone, then evaporation and deposit. PSO: inertia plus own-best plus swarm-best.
  4. Decay is as important as reinforcement. Without it, the first answer wins, whether it's right or not.
  5. LLM agents are few, expensive and correlated. Borrow the mechanisms (decaying shared state, explore vs exploit, negative feedback), not the hope of emergence.
