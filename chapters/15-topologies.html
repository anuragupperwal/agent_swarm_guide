Multi-agent topologies

Once you have more than one agent, you have to decide two things: who decides what happens next, and who is allowed to talk to whom. Every named pattern (pipeline, orchestrator, mesh, blackboard, debate, market) is one answer to those two questions. Learn the questions and the patterns stop being a list to memorise.

The big idea

A topology is the shape of a multi-agent system: which agents exist, how they are connected, and where control lives. It is the multi-agent equivalent of choosing a data structure. Most of the system's behaviour, good and bad, follows from it.

Two questions separate the topologies:

Control
Who decides which agent acts next? Fixed code, one coordinating agent, a tree of coordinators, or the agents themselves?
Communication
Who can send information to whom? Only along fixed edges, only up and down a tree, through a shared store, or anyone to anyone?
Key idea

Centralized control is easy to reason about, trace and budget, but the centre is a bottleneck and a single point of failure. Decentralized control is flexible, but you lose the ability to say in advance what the system will do. Most production systems sit near the centralized end and borrow one decentralized pattern where they need it.

The map

Static: pipeline and router

In these two, control lives in your code. Agents (or plain LLM calls) fill fixed slots. Strictly, chapter 02 would call them workflows rather than multi-agent systems; they belong here because many “multi-agent” products are exactly this.

Pipeline. Fits work with a known order: extract, then transform, then write. MetaGPT (2023) is the best-known LLM example: product manager, architect, project manager, engineer and QA engineer pass structured documents down an assembly line. Breaks when an early stage is wrong, because every later stage builds faithfully on the error (chapter 14's pn). Put a check between stages.

Router. Fits inputs that fall into distinct categories that each need different handling. Cheapest topology: only one specialist runs. Breaks when an input belongs to two categories, or the classifier is wrong and no one downstream notices. Log the routing decision; it is the first thing to check when an answer is bad.

Centralized: orchestrator-worker and hierarchy

Here an agent, not your code, decides the subtasks at run time. The orchestrator (also called a lead, manager or supervisor) reads the task, decides how to split it, hands parts to workers, and synthesizes what comes back. Workers talk only to the orchestrator, never to each other. OpenAI's agent guide calls this the manager pattern: the workers are exposed to the manager as tools.

Orchestrator-worker. Fits wide tasks whose subtasks can't be known in advance: research, multi-file investigation. Anthropic's research system (chapter 14) is this shape. Breaks when the plan is wrong (no worker can see the big picture to object), or when the orchestrator's window fills with results it must synthesize. The orchestrator is a single point of failure and a bottleneck.

Hierarchical. Fits tasks with natural sub-groupings too large for one orchestrator to hold, the way a company has teams. Failures are contained in a subtree. Breaks when depth grows: every level is another summary (information loss) and another link in the chain (compounding), and the number of agents grows exponentially with depth (chapter 16).

Note

Notice that orchestrator-worker is hierarchical with depth 1, and hierarchical is orchestrator-worker applied recursively. Whether it is one level or several is a parameter of the same design, which is why your harness can treat recursion depth as a limit rather than a separate architecture.

Decentralized: mesh and handoff

Now control moves. No single agent owns the plan; the agents themselves decide who acts next.

Mesh. Every agent can message every other; LangGraph's documentation calls this a network architecture. It fits small groups exploring an open problem, and simulations of agent societies. Breaks when it grows: channels grow with the square of the agent count, messages can loop forever, no agent owns termination, and a trace becomes a tangle. Rare in production for good reason.

Handoff. An agent decides that another agent should take over and transfers the conversation. OpenAI's experimental Swarm library (2024) made this its core idea, a tool that returns another agent; the OpenAI Agents SDK (2025) kept handoffs as a primitive. Fits conversations that move between specialists. Breaks when agents bounce a user back and forth, or the history passed along is too long or too thin.

Key idea

Agent-as-tool vs handoff is the most important distinction in this chapter. With agent-as-tool, the caller stays in charge: it calls a child, waits, gets a result back and carries on. With a handoff, the caller gives up control and the conversation now belongs to someone else. Your harness's subagents are tools. Ask yourself whether you will ever need the other kind.

Shared medium: the blackboard

The blackboard is the oldest pattern here. It comes from 1970s speech-understanding research, most famously the Hearsay-II system (survey published 1980). The picture is a room of experts around a blackboard: nobody gives orders; each expert watches the board and writes on it when it sees something it can improve.

The LLM version is a shared document, task board or scratchpad that several agents read and write. AutoGen's group chat (2023) is a close relative: every agent sees the shared transcript, and a manager chooses the next speaker. Fits problems solved by many small contributions from different experts, where the order isn't known in advance. Breaks when one bad entry pollutes everyone's view, when two agents overwrite each other (chapter 17's races), or when the board grows past what any one window can read. Its debugging story is good: the board's history is the trace.

Collective: debate and voting

These topologies don't split the work; they duplicate it. Several agents attempt the same question, and a protocol combines their answers.

  • Voting: agents answer independently and the majority wins. It is self-consistency (chapter 05) spread across agents.
  • Debate: agents see each other's answers, argue, and revise over several rounds. Du et al. (2023) reported better factuality and reasoning this way. Liang et al. (2023) added a judge and deliberately opposed positions to counter degeneration of thought, where a single model stays stuck on its first answer.

Fits questions with a checkable or arguable answer: maths, factual claims, reviewing a plan. Breaks when the agents share blind spots (the same model with the same prompt makes correlated errors, so votes are not independent), or when agents defer to whoever sounds most confident. Chapter 14's 1 − (1 − p)k assumes independence; debate deliberately removes it.

Market: the contract net

The contract net protocol (Reid G. Smith, 1980) organizes agents like a tender process. A manager announces a task; contractors that can do it send bids; the manager awards the task to the best bid; the winner reports the result. Any agent can be a manager for one task and a contractor for another.

Fits pools of heterogeneous agents whose capabilities or load change: different tools, different costs, some busy. Breaks when bids can't be trusted. A classic software contractor knows its queue length; an LLM agent asked “how well can you do this?” will often answer confidently and wrongly. If you try a market, make bids out of facts the harness can check (tools available, current load, past success rate) rather than self-assessment.

Why the channel count matters

With a hub, adding an agent adds one channel. With a full mesh, adding the n-th agent adds n − 1 channels, because it can talk to everyone already there. Each channel is something that can carry a wrong message, loop, or need tracing.

hub (star or tree): n − 1   ·   full mesh: n(n − 1)/2   ·   10 agents: 9 vs 45

Hybrids, and where your project sits

Real systems combine patterns. A common shape is a centralized tree for control, with a collective pattern inside one node (a vote to verify an answer) and sometimes a shared store on the side so siblings can see each other's findings without messaging each other directly.

In your swarm harness
  • Where you are. Your plan is a recursive orchestrator-worker tree with spawning as a tool. Put it on Figure 15.1. What does that position buy you for Stages 1–6 (tracing, budgets, termination)?
  • What you give up. List at least three things your tree cannot do. For each, say which Experiment 001 trace would show it first. (Hint: think about siblings, the root's plan, and the root's window during synthesis.)
  • Stage 7. It is conditional, and planned as mesh communication. Before you build it: would a blackboard solve the same problem with n channels instead of n(n − 1)/2? Write down the specific trace evidence that would justify Stage 7 at all.
  • Agent-as-tool vs handoff. Is there any stage in your plan where a child should take over rather than return? If not, say so explicitly in your design notes; it is a decision, not an omission.

Check yourself

Answer each one out loud or on paper before you open it.

Recall What two questions place any topology on the map?

Who controls what happens next (code, one agent, a tree, or the agents themselves), and who can communicate with whom (fixed edges, up and down a tree, through a shared store, or anyone to anyone).

Recall What is the difference between agent-as-tool and a handoff?

With agent-as-tool, the caller keeps control: it calls the child, waits, gets a result and continues. With a handoff, control and the conversation move to the other agent and do not return.

Apply 10 agents. Channels in a full mesh, and through a hub?

Mesh: 10 × 9 / 2 = 45. Hub: 10 − 1 = 9.

Apply A support desk: conversations start general, then move to billing or technical staff, and stay there. Which topology?

A router or triage step at the start, then handoffs. The user should talk to one agent at a time, and the specialist needs the full history. Orchestrator-worker would add a middleman to every turn.

Design Your orchestrator's plans are sometimes wrong, and workers faithfully execute them. Name two topology changes that could catch it.

Options include a check stage between planning and spawning (pipeline with a gate), a critic or a small vote reviewing the plan before execution, or human approval for expensive plans. Each adds cost at one point instead of wasting it across every worker.

Design Sibling researchers keep repeating each other's searches. Mesh or blackboard?

A blackboard is usually cheaper and easier to trace: each sibling writes findings to one store and checks it before searching, giving n channels instead of n(n − 1)/2, and the store's history doubles as a trace. A mesh lets siblings negotiate, which you rarely need for de-duplication. The simplest option of all may be a better plan from the parent.

Interview Compare orchestrator-worker and blackboard.

Orchestrator-worker: one agent decides the subtasks and every result flows through it; easy to budget and trace, but the orchestrator is a bottleneck and a single point of failure. Blackboard: agents contribute to a shared state when they can help, and a control rule picks who acts next; flexible about order, but shared state can be polluted or raced. Choose orchestrator-worker when subtasks are separable; a blackboard when the solution builds up incrementally from different kinds of expertise.

Interview Why isn't a debate among three copies of the same model as good as three independent votes?

Copies of the same model share training and blind spots, so their errors are correlated; and in a debate they read each other's answers, which removes independence on purpose. They can converge on a confident wrong answer. Diversity (different models, prompts or evidence) and a separate checker help.

Recall card

Five lines to rehearse. If you can expand each into a minute of explanation, you know this chapter.

  1. A topology answers two questions: who controls, and who talks to whom.
  2. Static (pipeline, router) → centralized (orchestrator, hierarchy) → decentralized (handoff, market) → collective (blackboard, debate) → mesh.
  3. Agent-as-tool returns control to the caller; a handoff gives it away.
  4. Hub channels grow as n − 1; mesh channels as n(n − 1)/2. Ten agents: 9 vs 45.
  5. Production systems are hybrids: a central tree for control, plus voting or a shared store where they help.
